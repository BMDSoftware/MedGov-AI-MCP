\begin{abstract}
Clinical AI tools for medical imaging are increasingly capable but remain largely isolated from one another, requiring custom integration work that slows adoption and creates fragmented, difficult-to-maintain deployments. This paper presents MedGov-AI, an agentic AI system that addresses the integration and orchestration problem directly by using the Model Context Protocol (MCP) as a standardized interoperability layer. Rather than hardcoding workflow logic, MedGov-AI uses a large language model as an autonomous orchestrator that discovers and invokes clinical tools at runtime through a set of MCP servers covering DICOM parsing, CT segmentation (MONAI), whole-slide image cell segmentation (Cellpose), radiology report generation (RadLex), and digital pathology (iPath). Domain-specific workflow governance is provided through skill files, plain-text protocol documents that encode step-by-step clinical sequences and guard conditions without retraining the model. The system supports both interactive and event-driven operation: in workspace monitoring mode, files deposited into a configured directory trigger autonomous agent execution without any user input. Three end-to-end use cases were evaluated: DICOM-based CT analysis with structured report generation; interactive whole-slide pathology tumor detection via iPath with cell segmentation and structured pathology reporting; and autonomous workspace monitoring with Cellpose cell segmentation and cross-workspace file routing. Results show that API-hosted large language models complete multi-step clinical workflows reliably within a bounded iteration budget, while locally-hosted 7-8B parameter models do not, revealing a current tension between privacy and reliability in clinical deployment. Skill-guided execution prevented domain-incorrect tool selection in the pathology use case, confirming that skills are necessary at decision forks where plausible but clinically incorrect options exist. Assisted and autonomous modes produced identical tool sequences, validating unattended autonomous operation. Six MCP servers were integrated without any agent-side connector code: each server wraps its underlying tool in the MCP protocol, and the agent discovers and invokes all of them through the same standard interface, supporting the argument that MCP can reduce the integration overhead that currently limits clinical AI adoption.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{What AI tools are deployed in clinical settings and what do they do?}
Several AI tools are currently used across clinical disciplines, with the notable characteristic that they typically operate in isolation from one another, with no unified integration layer. In radiology, AI assists with worklist prioritization and critical finding alerts, where the system detects life-threatening conditions such as stroke, pulmonary embolism, or intracranial hemorrhage and immediately notifies the clinical team. In screening programs, AI acts as a first filter, flagging high-risk cases for specialist review and allowing programs to scale without proportionally increasing the number of specialists required. Google Health conducted a published clinical trial of an AI system for diabetic retinopathy screening in Thailand, demonstrating that AI-assisted grading matched or exceeded specialist performance in a real clinical setting~\cite{ruamviboonsuk2022retinopathy, wolf2024access}. Mayo Clinic (United States) has integrated AI into ECG interpretation and cardiac imaging workflows as a decision support layer for cardiologists~\cite{mayoclinic-ai-cardiology}. In pathology, whole slide image analysis is used for tumour grading and cell counting, and AI-generated preliminary reports from imaging findings are now in active clinical use. For segmentation and detection specifically, FDA-cleared tools such as Viz.ai~\cite{vizai} and Aidoc~\cite{aidoc} are deployed for CT-based triage; Transpara~\cite{transpara} for mammography screening; and IDx-DR~\cite{idxdr} for diabetic retinopathy grading from fundus images.

\textbf{What are the benefits and what are the risks?}
The benefits of AI in clinical workflows are directly tied to the limitations of the current manual process. Faster analysis is the most immediate gain: AI systems can process and flag findings in seconds, reducing the time between acquisition and clinical decision. Consistency is a second key advantage, as AI models apply the same criteria uniformly across every case, unlike human specialists who are subject to fatigue, experience, and workload. Reducing routine reads and preliminary reporting also frees specialists for cases that genuinely require expert judgement. Taken together, these properties position AI not as a replacement for clinicians but as a force multiplier. The risks are equally significant. Bias is a primary concern: models trained on data from specific demographics may underperform when applied to different patient populations or institutions, since differences in demographics and treatment practices affect how well a model generalises~\cite{mittermaier2023bias}. In medical imaging specifically, bias can be introduced at any stage of the AI pipeline - from dataset composition to model training and deployment - leading to systematic errors that negatively affect generalisation and can compromise patient outcomes~\cite{kocak2025bias}. Hallucination is critical in language models used for report generation, where confident but clinically incorrect statements carry direct patient risk~\cite{griot2025metacognition}. The black-box nature of deep learning raises accountability concerns, as clinicians and regulators often cannot explain why a model reached a particular conclusion~\cite{kondylakis2026futureai}. From a regulatory standpoint, no unified framework governs how AI tools in healthcare should be validated, updated, or audited in production; Jia et al.~\cite{jia2025deployment} further showed that regulatory approval at development time does not cover new hazards that emerge only after deployment. Finally, and most relevant to this work, no established standards govern how agentic AI systems should operate in clinical environments, including how they communicate with existing tools, when they escalate to a human, and how their decisions are logged for audit~\cite{trustworthyai2025}. The gap between technical capability and clinical deployment is substantial: Cozzolino et al.~\cite{cozzolino2025surveillance} found that fewer than 4\% of AI studies had been evaluated in real clinical settings, and In Australia and New Zealand, over 80\% of radiologists had not integrated AI into their daily practice~\cite{kondylakis2026futureai}; a survey of radiologists in Lombardy, Italy found that fewer than 40\% reported daily AI use~\cite{ce2024perceptions}.


Despite the progress of individual AI tools, no standard integration layer exists to orchestrate them together within a clinical workflow. Each institution must develop its own connectors and its own standards, which leads to fragmentation, lack of interoperability, slow adoption, and deployments that are expensive and difficult to maintain. The Model Context Protocol (MCP)\footnote{MCP is an open protocol that defines a standardized client-server interface through which an AI agent discovers and invokes external tools. Each MCP server declares its available tools at startup with names, input schemas, and natural language descriptions; the agent selects and calls them by name at runtime without any prior knowledge of the server's implementation.} is a promising solution to this problem, providing a standardized way for agents to discover and call tools without custom integration. MCP has begun attracting application in clinical AI contexts~\cite{transformingclinical2026, kalathas2026mcp, ehtesham2025mcpfhir}. This work extends that direction to multi-tool clinical imaging orchestration.

Agentic AI\footnote{An agentic AI system is one that pursues a goal through iterative reasoning and tool use rather than producing a single response. At each step the model decides what action to take next, executes it via an external tool or API, observes the result, and continues until the goal is reached or the iteration budget is exhausted.} refers to systems that can autonomously set goals, plan multi-step actions, and use tools to achieve those goals~\cite{collaco2026scoping}. Unlike traditional AI models that operate in a single step or require human input at every stage, agentic systems can reason through complex tasks, make decisions about which tools to use, and adapt their behavior based on feedback. The terminology is not standardized: some authors use agentic AI to refer specifically to multi-agent orchestration systems, while others apply the term to any system capable of autonomous, multi-step action through tool invocation~\cite{abouali2026survey}. This work adopts the broader definition, treating MedGov-AI as an agentic system despite using a single-agent architecture. The application of agentic AI in healthcare is still in an early stage, with systematic reviews identifying very few systems evaluated in real clinical settings~\cite{collaco2026scoping, dietrich2025agentic}. Nevertheless, the case for AI assistance in clinical workflows rests on properties that are intrinsic to computational systems and have proven their value across domains from software engineering to image analysis: computers do not fatigue, apply criteria uniformly regardless of workload or time of day, and can process information faster than any human expert. Agentic systems extend these properties to multi-step reasoning, suggesting they are strong candidates for clinical imaging workflows, which follow defined protocols, involve repetitive steps with predictable structure, and produce well-defined outputs - properties that make them amenable to systematic, autonomous orchestration.

Three core properties characterize agentic systems: goal persistence (the system does not stop until the required deliverables are met), tool use (it can act on external systems, not just generate text), and iterative reasoning (each result informs the next decision). We evaluated several existing agentic frameworks, including LangChain~\cite{langchain}, CrewAI~\cite{crewai}, and AutoGen~\cite{autogen}, but opted to build a custom agentic system. The primary reason was the need for tight integration with MCP: existing frameworks do not natively support MCP as a tool protocol, which would have required additional adapter layers and reintroduced the fragmentation problem we were trying to solve. Building a custom loop also allowed us to enforce specific behaviors required in a clinical context, such as a completion contract under which the agent cannot declare success while required deliverables remain unmet, and a debug mode in which the user must approve each tool call before execution. The system uses a single-agent architecture; Collaco et al. note that single-agent designs offer greater simplicity, more predictable behavior, and clearer accountability for decisions compared to multi-agent systems~\cite{collaco2026scoping}, properties that are particularly important in a clinical context where every tool call must be traceable.

Contrasting with simpler alternatives clarifies why this design was necessary.
A plain chatbot produces one response per prompt and cannot call tools or take actions in the world. A hardcoded pipeline can call tools but relies on the developer to anticipate every possible execution path, making it brittle in the face of real-world variability. A standalone ML model provides predictions but has no language interface and cannot adapt its behavior based on context. We found that only a true agentic system could handle the complexity and variability of real clinical workflows, which often require conditional logic, error handling, and dynamic tool selection.

Our own system began as precisely this kind of hardcoded pipeline: in the initial version, the LLM was never consulted, and all sequencing decisions were made by explicit conditional logic in the orchestrator code. This approach proved limiting, as it could not adapt to new information without significant reprogramming, which motivated the transition to a fully agentic design.

This transition is not without its challenges. Reliable agentic behavior requires careful prompt engineering: the agent must be guided to select tools in the correct order, avoid premature declarations of success, and handle ambiguous or incomplete results gracefully. These challenges are discussed in detail in later sections.
However, the fundamental advantage over any hardcoded alternative is scalability: supporting a new clinical domain requires only an MCP server exposing the relevant tools - community-contributed servers already exist for established standards such as FHIR~\cite{ehtesham2025mcpfhir}, and where none exists, writing one is a self-contained effort isolated to that domain. We wrote our own servers to retain control over tool descriptions and add domain-specific operations not covered by existing implementations. The agent discovers and incorporates new servers at runtime without changes to the orchestration layer. This also opens a much wider architectural design space. Rather than a single fixed agent, one could configure specialist agents for distinct clinical tasks, use prompt-based behavioral switches to change the agent's operating mode, or compose multiple MCP servers covering entirely different domains.
None of these extensions require touching the orchestration code, which is not true of any hardcoded pipeline.

When testing the system we explored two LLM backend approaches: a locally-hosted model and a cloud API. The local model was Ollama's deepseek-r1:7b~\cite{deepseekr1} (4.7~GB), with llama3.1:8b~\cite{llama31} (4.9~GB) also evaluated, both small enough to run on consumer hardware and allowing for complete data privacy since all processing happens locally. The local approach proved to struggle with the demands of multi-step clinical workflows. We observed consistent failures in tool calling: the model frequently failed to select the correct tool for the given context and could not reliably chain multiple calls to reach a goal. Addressing this consumed considerable development time across prompt engineering, workflow simplification, logging, and targeted adjustments. None of these fully resolved the problem, as the root cause was model scale rather than configuration: smaller models have fewer parameters and correspondingly less capacity for the multi-step contextual reasoning required to chain tool calls reliably across a clinical workflow. Beyond raw capability, API-hosted models also represent a more accessible deployment path for clinical institutions. Acquiring and maintaining the GPU infrastructure required to run large local models is a significant investment that many healthcare organizations cannot justify, whereas access to a capable cloud model requires only an API subscription, a constraint independently confirmed across the field~\cite{agiformedicalimaging2025}. Switching to the API-hosted model resolved all of these issues immediately. The API approach introduces trade-offs of its own: latency, potential data security concerns in privacy-sensitive deployments, and a dependency on third-party service availability that is outside the institution's control. If the API experiences downtime or degraded performance, the entire orchestration layer is affected, which is a meaningful reliability concern in clinical settings. Despite these limitations, for the complex reasoning required by clinical imaging workflows it proved to be the only reliably viable option at the model sizes currently available for local inference. The system architecture supports both backends, allowing deployment to be tailored to the constraints of a given clinical environment. A recurring requirement across the agentic AI literature is seamless, secure interoperability with existing clinical platforms — Dietrich et al. identify this as a prerequisite for agentic AI to function without disrupting care delivery~\cite{dietrich2025agentic} — a problem that MCP directly addresses, as described in the following section.

\subsection{MCP as a Standardized Tool Integration Layer}

The conventional approach to agent-tool integration is the custom REST adapter: for each tool the agent must call, a custom connector is written that handles authentication, request serialization, response parsing, and error mapping. This approach does not scale. Each new tool adds a new adapter; changes to a tool API break the corresponding adapter; and the agent itself must encode assumptions about every tool it might call.

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) addresses this directly. MCP defines a client-server protocol in which each tool server declares its available tools at startup, specifying names, input schemas, and descriptions. The agent discovers tools dynamically at runtime and invokes them by name without requiring any prior knowledge of the server's internal implementation. This decouples the agent from individual tool APIs: adding a new tool server requires no changes to the agent, only a new MCP-compliant server that exposes the relevant capabilities.

Singh et al. (2025) survey MCP's architecture in detail, positioning it as the solution to fragmented API integrations for agentic AI systems~\cite{mcp-aditi}. The survey also identifies MCP sampling, a mechanism for LLM-to-LLM communication within the MCP protocol, as a path toward sub-agent specialization, in which a coordinating agent can delegate to specialist agents exposed as MCP servers. Singh et al. note that healthcare is a domain where these properties are especially relevant, but the survey discusses clinical applications only conceptually; no implemented system is presented. A preliminary working integration~\cite{mcphosts2025} demonstrated 95\% correct tool selection from natural language queries and confirmed MCP's role as a secure interoperability layer. Conceptually, MCP has been proposed as a foundational layer enabling context-aware, scalable integration of AI into clinical workflows~\cite{transformingclinical2026}, but production-grade clinical deployments remain scarce.

\subsection{Skills as Clinical Capability Packages}

Tool access via MCP determines what an agent can do, it does not determine what the agent should do in a given clinical domain. A segmentation server, a terminology server, and a report template server can all be exposed as MCP tools, but the correct sequencing of those tools for a CT lung evaluation versus a pathology workflow requires domain knowledge that is separate from both the agent's general reasoning capability and the tool's own logic.

Skills\footnote{In this context, a skill is a plain-text file that encodes domain-specific behavioral guidance for the agent - step-by-step workflow protocols, guard conditions, sequencing constraints, or bodies of domain knowledge such as regulatory standards or API usage patterns. Skills are not executable code; they are instructions that the agent reads and follows using its existing tools. The agent loads a skill on demand rather than at startup, keeping the system prompt minimal until a specific domain is required.} address this layer. Xu and Yan (2026) formalize agent skills as composable, on-demand capability packages that provide domain-specific behavioral guidance to an LLM without retraining it~\cite{agentskillslargelanguage}. The key architectural insight is that skills and MCP are complementary rather than competing: skills define what to do in a given domain, while MCP exposes the tools with which to do it. Xu and Yan do not address healthcare-specific skill design, the governance question of who maintains skill files when clinical protocols change, or how skills compose with MCP in a working system, gaps that this work directly addresses.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Existing work addresses components of this problem in isolation. Singh et al.~\cite{mcp-aditi} and~\cite{mcphosts2025} evaluate MCP as an integration protocol but do not demonstrate it in real clinical imaging workflows. Conceptual frameworks~\cite{transformingclinical2026} identify clinical information fragmentation and single-purpose tool limitations as core obstacles to clinical AI adoption but stop short of a working implementation. Work on medical imaging AI~\cite{agiformedicalimaging2025} focuses on model accuracy rather than orchestration, and confirms that deploying capable models locally in clinical institutions is a structural constraint rather than an isolated engineering problem. The skills approach for agentic systems has been formalized conceptually~\cite{agentskillslargelanguage} but not applied to clinical workflow governance or evaluated against a no-skill baseline.

MedGov-AI addresses the integration and orchestration layer directly: it combines DICOM parsing, segmentation inference, and structured report generation under a single agentic orchestrator using MCP as the interoperability layer and skill files to encode domain-specific workflow protocols. Both the MCP integration and the skill-based workflow governance are evaluated in end-to-end clinical workflows, providing empirical evidence for properties that prior work has only argued conceptually.

MedGov-AI is organized into three layers: a React frontend, a FastAPI
orchestrator, and a set of MCP tool servers. Each layer has a clearly
defined responsibility, and the boundaries between them are designed so
that clinical domain logic lives neither in the interface nor in the
transport layer but entirely in the orchestrator, where it can be
reasoned about, logged, and audited.

\subsubsection*{Frontend}
The client layer is a React~18 application that provides the clinical
user interface, communicating with the orchestrator exclusively via a
REST and SSE API\@. SSE enables real-time streaming of task progress
and agent responses to the user without polling.

\subsubsection*{Orchestrator}
The orchestrator is a FastAPI application and is the system's single
point of control: it exposes the REST and SSE API consumed by the
frontend, owns all session and task state, manages connections to every
MCP server, and hosts the three internal services that drive the
system's behaviour: the \texttt{Agent}, the
\texttt{task\_runner}, and the \texttt{watcher\_service}.

The \texttt{Agent} is the sole decision-maker in the system.
We use a single agent rather than a hierarchy of sub-agents for two reasons: simplicity and auditability.
Multi-agent coordination introduces inter-agent communication, shared state synchronisation, and context propagation across independently reasoning models - challenges documented in LLM-based multi-agent systems~\cite{guo2024llmma}. Our system targets a different regime: a single clinician submitting one request at a time, where task partitioning offers no benefit. A single agent suffices and already achieves model specialisation through MCP: inference tools delegate to MONAI and Cellpose, and the skills mechanism allows the agent to invoke additional task-specific models as needed - without the overhead of coordinating independent reasoning agents. Every tool call, its inputs, and its result are recorded in a single session trace, keeping the system straightforward to audit~\cite{collaco2026scoping}.
The agent runs an iterative LLM loop
using either the Gemini API or a locally-hosted Ollama model as its
reasoning backend, and executes function calling across multiple turns, each turn being one prompt-and-response cycle with the model, to invoke
MCP tools in sequence. It maintains a session context across turns so
that information produced by one tool call is available to the next
without requiring the user to restate it.

The \texttt{task\_runner} manages long-running operations that cannot
complete within a synchronous HTTP request. Operations such as MONAI
inference or Cellpose segmentation are submitted to a background thread
pool and tracked through a state machine with four states: queued,
running, completed, and failed.
A semaphore limits concurrent GPU
inference to a single job at a time, preventing out-of-memory crashes
on constrained hardware.
The task runner also captures the standard error streams
from inference processes and, on failure, passes the log to the LLM
for a plain-language error explanation before surfacing it to the user.
Task state is persisted in SQLite so that progress survives backend
restarts, and the frontend is notified of state transitions via SSE\@.

The \texttt{watcher\_service} monitors registered filesystem
directories using periodic polling and triggers the agent automatically
when a new file is deposited.
This enables event-driven clinical
workflows in which files arriving from a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) or a scanner
initiate the full analysis pipeline without any user action. No changes
to upstream systems are required; the watcher integrates at the
filesystem level and streams its activity log to the frontend via a
dedicated SSE channel.

All persistent state is stored in a single SQLite database with write-ahead logging. The schema covers users, sessions, conversation history,
uploaded files, background task status, watched directories, and
per-user tool settings.

\subsubsection*{LLM Backends}
The orchestrator supports two LLM backends, selectable at deployment
time without modifying any agent logic.
The Gemini API provides a
large cloud-hosted model capable of reliable multi-step function
calling in complex clinical workflows, at the cost of external data
transmission and dependence on third-party service availability.
Ollama provides a local inference
alternative using smaller open-weight models running entirely on the
deployment host with no external data transmission, which is relevant
in privacy-sensitive clinical environments, but proved insufficient for
reliable tool calling at the 7--8B parameter scale currently available
for local inference on ordinary hardware.

\subsubsection*{MCP Server Layer}
The tool layer consists of six MCP servers, each running as an
independent process in its own Python virtual environment.
Isolation at the process level is a deliberate design choice: it
prevents dependency conflicts between servers with incompatible
requirements (most critically between the GPU-enabled PyTorch
installation required by \texttt{mcp-monai} and the CPU-only
installation used elsewhere, which cannot coexist in a single
environment).
Tool names are automatically prefixed with the server name by the tool registry
(e.g., \texttt{monai.run\_inference}, \texttt{radlex.generate\_report}),
providing unambiguous namespacing across servers regardless of what any individual server declares.

\subsection{MCP Server Layer}

Each MCP server encapsulates a distinct clinical capability and exposes
it as a set of named, schema-defined tools. The agent has no hardcoded
knowledge of any server's internals. It discovers available tools at
startup and selects among them based on context.

\textbf{Mcp-monai} is the medical image inference server, built on the
MONAI framework~\cite{monai} with PyTorch as the underlying deep
learning backend. It detects the imaging modality and body part
automatically from pixel statistics and file metadata, returning a
ranked list of recommended models. The model catalogue can be filtered
by modality, body part, or category, and bundles are retrieved from
HuggingFace Hub and cached locally on demand. Inference uses a sliding
window strategy to handle the variable dimensions of 3D medical volumes. Bundled models cover spleen segmentation on CT, pancreas and
tumour segmentation on CT, lung nodule detection on CT, brain tumour
segmentation on multi-channel MRI, whole-body CT segmentation across
104 anatomical structures, and kidney segmentation using the UNEsT
architecture.

\textbf{Mcp-radlex} provides structured radiology reporting through
integration with the RadLex terminology standard.
The agent can browse
available radiological subspecialties, and search the template catalogue by
subspecialty or keyword. When populating a report from raw inference
results, the server uses MCP sampling to delegate the mapping of
segmentation outputs to template fields back to the host LLM, which
also generates the findings narrative, impression, and recommendations.
This closes the loop between inference output and clinical
documentation: the agent receives segmentation results, interprets
them, and produces a report conforming to a standard radiological
schema rather than free text.

\textbf{Mcp-utils} provides general-purpose filesystem and DICOM
operations that underpin every clinical workflow. It extracts the full
metadata of a DICOM file, including modality, body part, patient
identifiers, acquisition parameters, and pixel data statistics, and can
apply the same extraction across an entire directory. On the filesystem side, the

\subsection{Skills System}
\label{sec:skills}

The skills system provides a mechanism for encoding domain-specific
behavioral guidance that the agent loads on demand. A skill body can
take different forms depending on the domain: a step-by-step workflow
protocol with sequencing constraints and cross-server coordination, or
a body of domain knowledge such as regulatory standards, clinical
report templates, and API usage patterns. What these forms share is
that the guidance cannot be derived from MCP tool schemas alone and is
too domain-specific or voluminous to embed permanently in the system
prompt.
Embedding it permanently would increase token consumption on
every request, including those that do not require that domain, and
would increase the risk of the LLM overlooking or misapplying
instructions buried in a large prompt. Skills address
both problems by keeping the system prompt minimal and loading domain
detail only when the agent determines it is needed for the current
task.

\subsubsection{Structure}
A skill separates awareness from detail. Every available skill is
surfaced to the agent at startup through a short name and natural
language description kept permanently in the system prompt, while the
full skill body remains unloaded until needed. When a request arrives,
the agent reasons over these descriptions to identify the applicable
skill, then loads its body on demand as operating instructions for that
task. This enables progressive disclosure: the context window carries
only a lightweight index at rest, expanding to include full domain
detail only for the domain currently in use.

A skill body can itself be hierarchical, containing an entry-level
document that routes to more specific sub-documents, reference
material, templates, and scripts, each loaded independently as the
task requires. This allows a single skill to cover a broad domain
without forcing the agent to load everything at once.

\subsubsection{Implemented Skills}

Four skills are currently deployed in the system. The
\textbf{dicom-analysis} skill encodes the tool sequence and guard
conditions for DICOM analysis ending in a radiology report; the
\textbf{wsi-tumor-detection} skill encodes the visual inspection and
cell-counting workflow for whole-slide pathology images. The
\textbf{clinical-reports} and \textbf{pydicom} skills are publicly
available community skills: clinical-reports encodes
regulatory standards and structured templates for clinical
documentation, and pydicom provides API guidance for reading, writing,
anonymizing, and manipulating DICOM files programmatically. The imaging
skills illustrate skills as workflow protocols; the community skills
illustrate skills as domain knowledge packages, showing that the same
mechanism covers both forms of guidance and that externally maintained
skills can be adopted without modification.


\subsubsection{Governance}

A key property of this design is that skill files are plain text and
can be updated without touching the agent, the MCP servers, or any
other part of the codebase. When a workflow step changes (a
different tool call order, a new guard condition, a revised output
format) the relevant reference document is edited and the agent
adopts the new behaviour immediately on the next session, with no
software deployment required. This separates workflow maintenance from
software development, which is relevant in clinical settings where the
people who understand the correct procedure are not necessarily the
people who maintain the codebase.

\subsection{Agentic Execution Loop}

The agent's operation is governed by an iterative execution loop that drives the system from a natural language goal to a set of completed deliverables, calling MCP tools at each step based on the LLM's reasoning about what to do next.

\subsubsection{System Prompt and Session Initialisation}

At the start of each session the agent receives a system prompt that establishes its role, its available tools, and its behavioural rules. The system prompt includes the list of available skills as a plain bulleted list of names and descriptions, injected at startup after dynamic discovery, so the agent knows what clinical domains it can operate in before any user message arrives.
It also includes explicit rules for tool usage: when to read a skill file before acting, when to queue a long-running operation to the background task runner rather than calling it synchronously, how to handle DICOM series directories versus individual files, and how to avoid repeating failed tool calls.
Two variants of the system prompt exist: one for autonomous mode, which omits tool call confirmation instructions, and one for assisted mode, which includes them.

\subsubsection{Iterative Reasoning and Tool Dispatch}

Each iteration of the loop follows the same structure. The agent receives a prompt describing the current goal, any available files, and the results of all prior tool calls in the session.
On the first iteration the prompt is minimal: the goal (system prompt) and any uploaded files.
On subsequent iterations the prompt also includes the results of all prior tool calls, which the agent evaluates before deciding its next action. The LLM responds with either one or more function calls, or a text response indicating that the goal is complete or that more information is needed. Multiple tool calls can be returned in a single LLM response and are executed sequentially within the same iteration. All results are collected and returned to the LLM in a single message before the next iteration begins. Function calls are parsed and dispatched to the appropriate MCP server via the tool registry, and the result is appended to the execution history.

A repetition guard addresses a failure mode observed during development in which the agent would attempt the same failing tool call repeatedly rather than trying an alternative approach, consuming iterations without making progress. The guard inspects the execution history before each tool call: if the same tool has failed twice in a row, the third attempt is blocked and an error message is injected into the history instructing the agent to try a different tool or approach.

\subsubsection{Built-in Tools}

Alongside the MCP tools discovered at startup, the agent has access to a set of built-in tools that are handled directly by the orchestrator rather than by any MCP server. \texttt{goal\_achieved} signals that the task is complete and carries a summary of the result; calling it is the only way for the agent to exit the loop successfully. \texttt{need\_more\_info} allows the agent to pause execution and ask the user a clarifying question when the goal is ambiguous or required inputs are missing. \texttt{queue\_task} submits a long-running operation to the background task runner and returns immediately, allowing the agent to respond to the user without blocking on inference. \texttt{list\_tasks} retrieves the current status of queued or running background tasks, allowing the agent to check whether a previously submitted inference job has completed before proceeding. These tools give the agent explicit control over its own lifecycle and over the background task queue without exposing those concerns as MCP tool servers.

\subsubsection{Operating Modes}

The system supports two operating modes. In \textbf{autonomous mode}, the agent executes tool calls without interruption from goal receipt to completion. This is the intended mode for event-driven workflows triggered by the workspace watcher, where no user is present to intervene.
In \textbf{assisted mode}, the loop pauses before each tool call and returns a \texttt{confirmation\_required} response to the frontend, which presents the proposed tool name and arguments to the user for approval or rejection before execution resumes.

\subsection{Background Task Queue}

Inference operations such as MONAI CT segmentation and Cellpose cell counting are long-running by nature, taking anywhere from seconds to several minutes depending on hardware and input size. Blocking the agent or the HTTP request while waiting for these operations to complete would make the system unresponsive to other users and interactions. To avoid this, all inference calls are offloaded to a background thread pool: when the agent calls a tool that queues inference, the tool returns immediately with a task identifier, and the actual computation runs asynchronously.

Each task follows a simple state machine: \texttt{queued} when submitted, \texttt{running} once a thread picks it up, and \texttt{completed} or \texttt{failed} on exit. Task state is persisted in a SQLite database so that results survive server restarts and can be retrieved by any session. A semaphore limits concurrent inference to a single job at a time, preventing simultaneous runs from exhausting GPU memory.
When a job finishes, an SSE notification is pushed to the frontend, which updates the results tab without requiring the user to poll or refresh. This design allows multiple users to submit jobs independently: each submission is queued and processed in order, with results delivered to the corresponding session on completion.



\subsection{Workspace Monitoring and Event-Driven Triggering}

Not all clinical workflows are initiated by a user interacting with a chat interface. In many settings, imaging files are deposited into shared directories as part of existing institutional processes, with no operator present to trigger analysis manually. The workspace monitoring system addresses this by turning filesystem events into agent triggers, allowing MedGov-AI to process incoming files automatically without requiring modification to upstream workflows.

\subsubsection{Architecture}

The system refers to each monitored directory as a \textit{workspace}. Each workspace is a named, registered directory stored in the SQLite database alongside a configurable prompt that defines what the agent should do when a file arrives. At startup, the orchestrator resumes watching all previously registered workspaces.
The \texttt{watcher\_service} uses a polling observer to monitor each registered path recursively, bridging filesystem events into the orchestrator's event loop via an internal queue.

When a new file is detected, the service does not trigger the agent immediately. Instead it starts a three-second debounce window: if additional files arrive within that window, they are batched together. Only after three seconds of inactivity does the service dispatch all accumulated files to the agent in a single invocation. This prevents a burst of files, such as a DICOM series arriving slice by slice, from spawning multiple redundant agent executions.

\subsubsection{Trigger Flow}

When the debounce window closes, the watcher stages the detected files in the workspace's \texttt{incoming/} subfolder and constructs a goal prompt that includes the workspace name, the list of received files, and the preconfigured prompt for that workspace. It then invokes the agent in autonomous mode, since no user is present to confirm tool calls.
The agent proceeds through its standard execution loop: reading the appropriate skill file(s) (if needed), parsing the files, running the required analysis, and producing the required outputs. The entire activity log, including file detection events, agent actions, and results, is streamed to the frontend via SSE under the workspace's dedicated console channel, giving an operator real-time visibility even when the workflow was triggered without user interaction.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Three clinical workflows were selected to evaluate the system. Together they cover the two imaging modalities the system targets, radiology and pathology, and exercise the full tool stack: DICOM parsing, segmentation inference, report generation, whole slide image analysis, and event-driven workspace monitoring. Use Case 1 targets radiology, while Use Cases 2 and 3 both target pathology on whole-slide images, differing in how they are triggered: interactively by a clinician and autonomously by the workspace monitor, respectively.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}

Evaluation was structured in three phases. Phase 1 evaluates both API-based and locally hosted language models for tool-calling reliability in clinical workflows. Phase 2 evaluates whether the agent can operate autonomously in clinical workflows. It first examines how skills provide the workflow constraints that make autonomous execution reliable, showing that the agent follows the correct tool sequence and guard conditions without human intervention. It then compares assisted and autonomous execution of the same task to assess whether human-in-the-loop confirmation changes the outcome, or whether the agent produces equivalent results in both modes. Phase 3 runs all three use cases end to end. Use Cases 1 and 2 are evaluated by task completion and qualitative assessment of outputs (whether the report was correctly filled and whether segmentation completed successfully). Use Case 3 is evaluated by end-to-end autonomous operation of the workspace monitoring pipeline.

Language model experiments were run on a laptop with an AMD Ryzen 5 8645HS CPU, 16~GB DDR5 RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 6~GB GPU. MONAI inference benchmarks were run on two configurations: the same local machine using CUDA (GPU), and a shared deployment server using CPU-only inference, using MONAI 1.5.1 and PyTorch 2.6.0.
Two categories of language model were tested: large API-based proprietary models (Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 3.0, with parameter counts undisclosed by Google) and locally hosted open-source models of significantly smaller scale (deepseek-r1:7b and llama3.1:8b, both at approximately 7--8 billion parameters, run via Ollama).

Success is defined as a completed segmentation run with a structured report produced without manual intervention.

\subsubsection{Setup}
The user provides a slide UID and enters the following prompt in the chat interface:

\begin{quote}

\subsubsection{Workflow}
The agent begins by calling \texttt{ipath.fetch\_thumbnail} with the slide UID to retrieve a scaled thumbnail of the whole-slide image, which is returned as an image the agent can inspect directly. It then calls \texttt{ipath.get\_slide\_dimensions} to retrieve the full slide dimensions, and \texttt{ipath.scale\_roi\_to\_slide} to map the thumbnail bounding box to the corresponding coordinates on the full-resolution slide. The high-resolution region of interest is extracted with \texttt{ipath.fetch\_roi}, which enforces a 2700~px maximum per side. \subsection{Use Case 3 - Autonomous Pathology ROI Monitoring}

\subsubsection{Clinical Context}
A pathology instrument or PACS system exports region-of-interest (ROI) images to a local directory as part of its standard output. No human is available to initiate analysis for each arriving file. The goal is for the system to detect each new file, run cell segmentation automatically, and route the result based on a configurable cell count threshold, without any user interaction. Files can also be deposited manually through the Workspaces tab in the frontend, which provides a console showing detection events and agent activity in real time. Success requires a completed segmentation run and correct routing of the result based on the configured cell count threshold.

\subsubsection{Setup}
A workspace is registered pointing to the monitored directory. The following prompt is configured for that workspace, defining the agent's behaviour for every arriving file:

\begin{quote}
\textit{``When a new file arrives, check whether it is a pathology ROI image. If it is, run Cellpose cell segmentation and count the cells. If the cell count exceeds 2000, copy the segmentation mask to the output folder and log the result. If the file is not a valid ROI image, move it to the uncertain folder.''}
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Workflow}
The workflow is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:workspace-workflow}.
When the polling observer detects a new file, the watcher stages it in the workspace's \texttt{incoming/} subfolder and triggers the agent after a three-second debounce window. The agent calls \texttt{get\_file\_metadata} to determine file type, extension, and dimensions, and attempts to classify whether the file is a pathology ROI image.
This classification step is an inherent limitation of the current design: reliably determining whether an arbitrary image is a valid ROI would require running a dedicated detection model, adding an inference step before any analysis begins. To avoid this overhead, the workspace prompt instructs the agent to make a best-effort classification from file metadata and dimensions alone, and to route uncertain cases to a dedicated folder for manual review rather than proceeding with segmentation. This trades false-negative robustness for efficiency, and is an acknowledged design constraint rather than a failure of the workflow. When the task completes, the result is pushed to the frontend via SSE and displayed in the Workspaces console. Final results, including cell count, mask path, and timestamp, are persisted and accessible in the Results tab.

\subsection{Phase 1 - Model Experimentation}
% Gemini vs Ollama for tool calling in clinical workflows

Five language models were evaluated: three large API-based models (Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 3.0)~\cite{gemini2025} and two locally-hosted open-source models (deepseek-r1:7b~\cite{deepseekr1} and llama3.1:8b~\cite{llama31}) run via Ollama. The agent loop was capped at 10 iterations per task, meaning a model that cannot reach \texttt{goal\_achieved} within 10 tool calls fails the task regardless of how many individual tool selections were correct.

\subsubsection{Local Models}

Both Ollama models were tested on a laptop with an RTX 3050 6~GB GPU at two power profiles: 30W and 60W. At 30W, tool selection was generally correct on the first attempt, and in the majority of cases the first tool called was the appropriate one for the context. However, neither model reliably reached \texttt{goal\_achieved} within the 10-iteration limit: the models would select correct individual tools but fail to chain them into a complete workflow, running out of iterations before producing a final result. At 60W, outcomes were mixed: some tasks completed successfully within the iteration budget, others did not. The observed difference between power profiles is consistent with reduced inference throughput at lower wattage, though a systematic characterization of this effect is outside the scope of this work. A consistent observation across both power profiles was that when a tool call failed, the local models rarely recovered by trying an alternative approach, confirming the need for the repetition guard described in Section~\ref{sec:skills}.

Each tool call with a locally-hosted model took approximately 20 seconds of inference time, making a 10-iteration task take up to three minutes of model time alone, before accounting for actual tool execution. This is a fundamental constraint of the model scale rather than a configuration issue.

A key finding from this phase is that two implementation choices significantly influenced local model reliability regardless of power profile: the quality of tool descriptions in each MCP server, and the availability of stateful execution history. Without clear, specific tool descriptions, the models defaulted to plausible-sounding but incorrect tool selections. Without execution history passed back at each iteration, the models repeated the same failing tool calls rather than adapting. Both of these are architectural properties of the system, not model-specific tunings, and both apply equally to the API-based backend.

\subsubsection{API-Based Models}

Gemini 2.0 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Flash resolved all of the reliability issues observed with local models. Both models completed multi-step clinical workflows within the iteration budget, correctly sequenced tool calls, and recovered from tool failures by selecting alternatives.
LLM inference per iteration was near-instant with API-based models, compared to the approximately 20~seconds required per iteration with local models, reducing full task completion time by an order of magnitude. MCP tool execution time is identical regardless of which LLM backend is used, since tools run locally in all configurations. The primary limitations of the API approach are external: API availability is outside the system's control, and all data submitted to the agent is transmitted to a third-party service, which is a concern in privacy-sensitive clinical environments. A further practical consideration is token consumption. Because the agent passes its full execution history back to the LLM at each iteration, token usage grows with every tool call in the loop. Two concrete examples from development illustrate the scale of this problem. In an early version, image bytes were passed directly to the LLM instead of a file path, resulting in a single analysis call consuming approximately 60,000 tokens for a 57~MB scan. Separately, the \texttt{utils.parse\_dicom\_directory} tool was including all file paths in its response, adding approximately 27,000 tokens per call for large directories. After fixing both issues, a representative multi-file task begins at roughly 1,720 prompt tokens on the first iteration and grows to approximately 2,352 by the second, reflecting the expected accumulation rate from execution history.

Several measures were introduced to manage this growth. The agent uses Gemini's native stateful session mode, meaning only the latest tool result is transmitted per turn rather than rebuilding the full history in each prompt. Report generation uses MCP sampling, a lateral LLM call that produces the report narrative without adding to the agent's context window.
The \texttt{get\_template\_schema} tool was removed in a subsequent refactor of the RadLex server, eliminating a manual template inspection step that consumed tokens on every report workflow.
The skills progressive disclosure mechanism avoids embedding domain knowledge permanently in the system prompt by loading it only when the agent determines it is needed for the current task. These values and the measures taken represent the state of the system at time of writing; further optimizations remain possible.

\subsubsection{Summary}

The results confirm that the system architecture is backend-agnostic and functions with both local and API-hosted models; the gap lies in task completion reliability at current local model scales. Local models at 7--8B parameters can select appropriate tools but cannot reliably complete multi-step workflows within a bounded iteration budget. API-hosted models at significantly larger scale resolve this reliably. The architecture supports both backends without modification, allowing deployment to be matched to the constraints of a given environment: a privacy-critical deployment can use a local model with the understanding that task complexity must be managed, while a deployment where API access is available benefits from reliable autonomous operation. This experiment evaluates whether loading a skill changes the agent's tool selection behaviour compared to operating without one, and whether the skill-prescribed sequence is actually followed in autonomous execution.

The \textbf{dicom-analysis} skill was used as the test case. Without the skill active, the agent can still complete a DICOM analysis task but selects tools based solely on its general reasoning and the tool descriptions available at startup. With the skill loaded, the agent receives an explicit step-by-step workflow: parse the DICOM header first, call \texttt{analyze\_image} to confirm modality and body part, call \texttt{list\_models} filtered to those parameters, download the model if not cached, and only then queue inference. Each step is a guard condition for the next: inference is not attempted until the model is confirmed to match the scan.

In autonomous execution with the skill active, the agent followed this sequence without deviation across all 10 test runs (10/10 success rate). Guard conditions were respected: on one occasion where \texttt{analyze\_image} returned an ambiguous modality, the agent called \texttt{list\_models} with both candidate modalities before selecting, rather than proceeding with the first result. Without the skill, the agent completed the task in 10/10 runs, though it occasionally skipped the analysis step and called \texttt{list\_models} directly with parameters inferred from the filename, which produced correct results in straightforward cases but would fail on files with non-descriptive names or atypical metadata.

A comparable observation was made in workspace monitoring execution. A deployment trace from March 2026 shows the agent autonomously handling an unrecognised file: it called \texttt{utils.move\_file}, attempted \texttt{monai.analyze\_image} (which returned a DICOM header error), created an \texttt{uncertain/} subdirectory, moved the file there, and called \texttt{goal\_achieved}, completing the task in six iterations without any user input. This confirms that the agent can execute multi-step conditional workflows autonomously and handle tool failures gracefully by adapting its plan rather than halting.

The conclusion from this phase is that skills are not required for the agent to complete tasks, but they are what makes autonomous execution \emph{reliable}: they constrain the agent to the correct domain-specific sequence and prevent it from taking plausible but clinically incorrect shortcuts.

A complementary observation concerns when the agent selects a direct MCP tool over a skill covering the same operation. When both options were available for the same task, the agent tended to prefer the direct tool. This is structurally expected: a direct MCP tool exposes its full name, schema, and description to the agent at startup, whereas a skill is known only by its short description until explicitly loaded. Any guidance buried within the skill's hierarchical structure --- sub-documents, templates, reference material --- is invisible to the agent until it decides to load them. When a direct tool already matches the task, the agent has no reason to load a skill whose contents it cannot yet see. For example, extracting DICOM metadata via the \texttt{utils.parse\_dicom} MCP tool requires one call, whereas the \texttt{pydicom} skill achieves the same result in three calls --- reading the skill entry point, loading references, and executing the script --- with additional context window usage. Skills are therefore most valuable where no direct MCP tool exists for the required operation, where workflow sequencing across multiple tools must be constrained, or where the domain knowledge to be loaded is too large or too domain-specific to embed permanently in the system prompt.

\subsubsection{Assisted vs Autonomous Mode}
% run the same task in both modes
% show the tool call sequence was identical in both
% conclusion: assisted mode is a transparency mechanism, not a correction mechanism
% this validates autonomous mode for event-driven workflows (Use Case 2)

The same DICOM analysis task was executed twice: once in assisted mode, where the agent paused before each tool call and presented the proposed tool name and arguments to the user for confirmation, and once in autonomous mode, where all tool calls were executed without interruption. The goal prompt, uploaded files, and active skill were identical in both runs.

The tool call sequences were identical across both executions. The agent selected the same tools in the same order, passed the same arguments, and reached \texttt{goal\_achieved} in the same number of iterations. No tool call was rejected or modified by the user during the assisted run, and the outputs produced, segmentation results and per-organ volumes in the Results tab, were equivalent.

This result has a direct architectural implication. If assisted mode were a correction mechanism, the two sequences would diverge whenever the user overrode a proposed tool call. The fact that they did not indicates that the agent's autonomous decisions were already correct, and that the confirmation step added visibility without changing the outcome.
Assisted mode is therefore best understood as a transparency mechanism for operators who want to inspect each tool call before it executes, rather than a safety net that catches errors the agent would otherwise make.
This in turn supports autonomous mode for event-driven workflows such as Use Case 3, where no user is present: the agent's decisions were correct independently of human oversight.




\subsection{Phase 3 - Use Case Comparison}

This phase evaluates the system through three end-to-end clinical workflows. Use Case 1 covers interactive DICOM analysis: a user uploads a CT series, issues a natural language prompt, and the agent autonomously parses the study, selects and runs a segmentation model, and generates a structured radiology report. Use Case 2 covers interactive WSI tumor detection: the user provides a slide UID, and the agent retrieves a region of interest from the iPath platform, runs cell segmentation via Cellpose, and generates a structured pathology report. Use Cases 1 and 2 were each executed under two conditions, with and without a skill loaded, to isolate the effect of domain-specific workflow guidance on agent behavior. Use Case 3 covers event-driven pathology analysis: the workspace monitoring system detects incoming ROI images, triggers the agent without user input, runs cell segmentation, and routes the result based on a configurable threshold rule.

The Cellpose v4 model (cpsam) is a SAM-based architecture designed for GPU execution. On CPU, inference took 95 seconds on a 64$\times$64 pixel test image. A real pathology ROI image at typical resolution (2{,}352$\times$2{,}673~px) completed in \textbf{12 minutes 37 seconds} on a local Ryzen 5 8645HS CPU, and ran for approximately three hours on the deployment server before hanging. The contrast with the MONAI inference times in Table~\ref{tab:inference-results} is notable: MONAI times are nearly identical across both machines because the server is configured with \texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1} and \texttt{MKL\_NUM\_THREADS=1}, pinning it to a single thread where per-core speed is comparable. Cellpose on CPU is unrestricted and scales with available cores; the deployment server runs as a virtual machine with only 4 cores allocated from a Ryzen 9 5950X host, introducing both core-count and VM overhead constraints that explain the order-of-magnitude difference relative to the local machine. On the local machine with an RTX 3050 GPU (CUDA 13.0), the same image completed in \textbf{30 seconds}.
On the deployment server with an RTX 3060 GPU (CUDA 12.1), the same image completed in \textbf{28 seconds}, confirming that GPU execution eliminates the VM CPU bottleneck entirely and yields equivalent performance across both hardware configurations. GPU execution is therefore not an optional accelerator but a practical prerequisite for the pathology use case~\cite{cellpose}. The MONAI inference pipeline is similarly GPU-dependent: on the test machine (RTX 3050 6~GB), certain models exceeded available VRAM and caused the subprocess to terminate without producing any output or error message. This failure mode was only diagnosed after adding stderr capture to the task runner, which then surfaced the underlying PyTorch out-of-memory exception. These findings shaped deployment requirements: the system is shipped with a GPU-enabled Docker configuration as the recommended deployment target, with CPU fallback available for development and testing only.

A practical advantage of both workflows, independent of accuracy, is the convenience the system provides. Processing speed depends on available hardware, cached models, and firmware, and is therefore highly deployment-specific. What the architecture guarantees regardless of hardware is that long-running operations are queued automatically, results are persisted, and the clinician can return to completed outputs without having been present during execution. In the workspace monitoring scenario this translates directly to overnight or unattended processing: files deposited at the end of a working day are analyzed and results are available the next morning.
Meaningful evaluation of this property at scale, however, requires a broader set of test datasets and input types covering diverse modalities, resolutions, and acquisition protocols, which represents one important direction for further experimental work.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{MCP as a Practical Integration Layer}

Section~\ref{sec:background} identifies fragmentation as a structural problem in clinical AI: tools exist, but no standard layer connects them, forcing each institution to build and maintain custom integrations. MCP was evaluated in this work as a candidate solution to that problem. The results support that framing. Six servers covering distinct clinical capabilities were integrated without any agent-side connector code: each server exposes its tools through the MCP protocol, and adding a new server required no changes to the agent or any existing component. More importantly, this composability held under real workflow conditions: all three use cases required the agent to sequence operations across multiple servers dynamically, and in all cases it did so correctly without any hardcoded knowledge of how those servers were implemented.

The broader implication is that MCP could reduce the integration overhead that currently slows AI adoption in clinical settings. Under the conventional approach, connecting a new tool to an existing workflow requires custom development work regardless of how well-designed the tool itself is. Under an MCP-based architecture, the integration contract is defined by the protocol: a conforming server is immediately accessible to any conforming agent. If this holds as more clinical tools adopt MCP, the bottleneck shifts from integration engineering to tool capability itself, which is a more tractable problem. Singh et al. argue this conceptually~\cite{mcp-aditi}; the use cases presented here provide evidence that it holds in practice.

One finding qualifies this picture. MCP decouples the agent from a server's implementation, but it does not decouple the agent's decision-making from the quality of each tool's natural language description. Vague or ambiguous descriptions caused incorrect tool selections even when the correct tool was available, an effect observed consistently with local models and to a lesser degree with API-based models. This means the integration problem is not fully solved by adopting MCP: it is partially displaced from adapter code to description authorship.
That is a smaller and more localised problem, but it is not insignificant. A further mitigation observed in this system is that skills partially compensate for this: when a skill is active, the agent follows an explicit protocol that names specific tools, reducing its dependence on description quality for sequencing decisions~\cite{agentskillslargelanguage}. MCP and skills are therefore complementary: MCP provides the integration layer, skills provide the domain knowledge that makes reliable use of that layer possible. Neither alone is sufficient for complex clinical workflows.

One open question is how this composability scales beyond the six servers evaluated here. The architecture imposes no limit on the number of registered servers, and adding a new one requires no changes to the agent. However, all tool descriptions are loaded into context at startup, and as the catalogue grows the agent's ability to select the correct tool from an increasingly large set is expected to degrade, a direct instance of the failure mode where options buried deeper in a long context are more likely to be overlooked or misapplied. Skills that function as workflow guides partially mitigate this by prescribing explicit tool sequences, reducing the risk of the agent selecting the wrong tool from an increasingly large catalogue, but they do not reduce the size of the tool list itself. A natural extension would be a multi-agent architecture in which specialist agents each expose only the tools relevant to their domain, keeping individual context windows bounded regardless of how many total servers exist. This design was not evaluated here and remains future work~\cite{klang2026orchestrated}.

\subsection{Skills as Workflows: When Guidance Changes Behaviour}

Use Cases 1 and 2 clarify when a skill operating as a workflow changes the agent's behaviour and when it does not. In Use Case 1, the skill made no difference to the outcome: the DICOM analysis workflow presents no ambiguous decision forks, so the agent reached the correct sequence through general reasoning alone. The skill enforces protocol adherence on top of an already correct execution, which matters for reproducibility but does not change the result. Use Case 2 produced a different outcome, with a clinically incorrect decision in the unskilled condition (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

The failure illustrates the mechanism by which a workflow skill corrects agent behaviour: a \emph{domain selection failure} in which the agent called \texttt{radlex.generate\_radiology\_report} on a pathology specimen. The reason follows from the tool-preference pattern observed in Section~\ref{sec:results}: \texttt{radlex.generate\_radiology\_report} is a directly callable MCP tool fully visible at startup, while the \texttt{clinical-reports} skill is opaque until loaded, its internal templates invisible until the agent decides to open it. Without a workflow directing that loading step, the agent selected the most visible tool matching the goal of generating a report, with no mechanism to detect the domain mismatch from descriptions alone. The skill resolved this by prescribing the loading of the correct template and explicitly ruling out the radiology tool, a routing decision the agent could not reach on its own.

The practical implication is that skills as workflows are most valuable at decision forks where a plausible but incorrect option exists: a choice between tools where domain appropriateness cannot be inferred from names and descriptions alone. Where no such fork exists, as in Use Case 1, the skill adds no behavioural change and its value is limited to reproducibility guarantees.

\subsection{Clinical Accuracy and Validation Scope}

The evaluation in this work measures task completion: whether the agent correctly orchestrated the required tools, followed the prescribed workflow, and produced an output. It does not measure clinical correctness: whether the segmentation outputs are accurate enough to inform a clinical decision. These are distinct properties, and conflating them would overstate what the system has been shown to do.

Segmentation accuracy for the models used (spleen, pancreas, and whole-body CT segmentation via MONAI) has been established in their respective publications and benchmarks. What has not been evaluated here is whether the agent's orchestration introduces errors that would not arise in a direct model invocation: whether the correct model is consistently selected for a given scan, whether the inference parameters passed by the agent are appropriate, and whether the structured report correctly maps segmentation findings to the right template fields. These are failure modes specific to the agentic layer, not the underlying models, and they require dedicated clinical validation to assess.

A rigorous evaluation would require comparison of agent-produced outputs against ground-truth annotations by trained radiologists, Dice similarity scores across a representative dataset of scan types and qualities, and testing across institutions with different equipment and acquisition protocols. Until that validation is done, the system should be understood as a workflow automation tool that reduces the manual effort of orchestrating clinical imaging pipelines, not as a clinically validated decision support system. That distinction is not a limitation unique to this work, as it applies to any system that introduces an agentic orchestration layer over existing models, but it needs to be stated explicitly.

\subsection{Privacy vs Reliability in Clinical Deployment}

The results expose a tension that has no clean resolution in the current system. While API-based models completed multi-step clinical workflows reliably, locally hosted models at 7--8B parameters did not. The practical consequence is that reliable autonomous operation currently entails transmitting clinical metadata, including patient identifiers present in DICOM headers, to a third-party cloud service, which is a significant concern in clinical environments governed by data protection regulations such as GDPR and HIPAA. The two properties that matter most for clinical adoption, privacy and reliability, pull in opposite directions under current model constraints.

This constraint reflects the computational resources available for this evaluation rather than the state of local model capability in general. Larger open-weight models capable of more reliable multi-step reasoning exist but require GPU resources beyond what was available in this work, and beyond what most clinical deployments can provide without dedicated AI infrastructure. The models evaluated locally represent what is practically deployable on standard institutional hardware, which is the relevant constraint for widespread clinical adoption. The architecture supports both backends without modification, which means the system can be deployed in privacy-critical environments using a local model. The limitation observed here is not inherent to local inference but specific to the 7--8B parameter models tested: a sufficiently capable open-weight model running locally would satisfy both requirements simultaneously. As hardware accessible to clinical institutions improves and larger open-weight models become practically deployable, the same architecture would benefit without any changes to the orchestration layer.

Several paths could close this gap. Fine-tuning a smaller model specifically on tool-calling sequences in clinical workflows could improve reliability without requiring frontier-scale parameters. On-premise deployment of larger models, where the hardware investment is justified by the privacy requirement, is a viable option for well-resourced institutions. A hybrid approach, where a local model handles simple routing decisions and an API model is invoked only for complex multi-step reasoning on de-identified data, could balance both concerns. None of these were evaluated in this work, and they represent concrete directions for future investigation~\cite{trustworthyai2025, dietrich2025agentic, agiformedicalimaging2025}.

\section{Conclusions}

This work set out to demonstrate that an agentic AI system using MCP as a standardized interoperability layer can autonomously orchestrate multi-step clinical imaging workflows without custom integration code. The results support that claim. All three use cases completed end-to-end without manual intervention, six MCP servers were integrated without agent-side connector code, and the skill-based governance mechanism demonstrably changed agent behavior in the pathology task, preventing domain-incorrect tool selection that occurred without it. The finding that assisted and autonomous modes produced identical tool sequences confirms that the agent's decisions were correct independently of human oversight, validating unattended operation for event-driven workflows.

The comparison between API-hosted and locally-hosted models exposed a tension that clinical deployment cannot currently avoid. Reliable multi-step tool-calling requires model scale that exceeds what most institutions can run locally, yet transmitting clinical data to a third-party API raises data protection concerns under GDPR and HIPAA. This is not a limitation unique to this system; it reflects the current state of open-weight model capability relative to the computational resources available in clinical environments~\cite{agiformedicalimaging2025}. A hybrid approach, where a local model handles routine decisions on identified data and an API model is invoked only for complex reasoning on de-identified inputs, represents a concrete direction for resolving this tension.

Logging and auditing, implemented here primarily as a debugging and transparency mechanism, proved to be more valuable than anticipated. The workspace monitoring console provided a step-by-step trace of every autonomous agent action, which was essential both for diagnosing failure modes and for building confidence in unattended operation. As agentic systems take on more consequential clinical tasks, this kind of decision traceability will become a compliance requirement rather than a convenience~\cite{trustworthyai2025, dietrich2025agentic}. Security considerations, including tool-level access control and audit trail integrity, were not the focus of this work but represent a necessary area of development before any production clinical deployment~\cite{transformingclinical2026}.

Several directions remain for future work. Token consumption optimization is the most immediate: further reducing context window growth through smarter execution history pruning and selective skill loading would lower API costs and extend the effective iteration budget. Prompt engineering and fine-tuning smaller models specifically on clinical tool-calling sequences could close the reliability gap between local and API backends without requiring frontier-scale compute. The current background task queue serializes inference for safety but is not designed for multi-tenant scale; a dedicated worker process per GPU using a proper task queue would be the right architecture for production deployment across multiple concurrent users. Finally, and most importantly, the evaluation presented here measures workflow orchestration correctness, not clinical accuracy. A rigorous validation requires Dice score comparisons against ground-truth annotations across a representative dataset, prospective testing across institutions with different equipment, and review by trained clinicians. That validation is the necessary next step before MedGov-AI can be considered for any real clinical use.

The broader contribution of this work is a demonstration that MCP, combined with a skills system for domain-specific governance, provides a practical and extensible integration layer for clinical AI. The approach is not limited to the servers implemented here: any clinical tool that can be wrapped in an MCP server becomes immediately accessible to the agent without changes to the orchestration layer. As the clinical AI ecosystem matures and more tools adopt MCP, the integration overhead that currently limits adoption~\cite{collaco2026scoping} shifts from adapter engineering to tool capability itself, which is a more tractable problem for the field to solve.
